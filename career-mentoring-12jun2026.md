# Career Mentoring

- **Mentor:** Steven
- **Subject:** IT, Software Engineering, Ethical Hacking, Secure Coding
- **Context:** Advice for High School students interested in computers, IT etc.

## 1. What does a typical day in your job look like?

I catch the bus into the office most days. I rarely work from home, but I can when I need to.

### Previous role: Senior Software Engineer

Stand-up once a day at 2pm. Why 2pm? It was a better time for our
colleagues in India. We'd go around each person, two to three minutes
each. Am I stuck? Do I need help? Can I finish today's task?

More meetings than you'd expect as a senior engineer. Project
meetings, consulting, reviewing, helping people when they need it.

### Current role: Senior DevOps Engineer

No stand-up. I'm in a team of two. We have a catch-up once a week,
and connect regularly through the day, either face to face or on chat.

Fewer meetings than my old team.

### Both roles

Most of the time my earbuds are in. Building the solution. Solving
the problem. Investigating. Fixing. Writing tests. Is it secure? Have
I introduced a vulnerability? If I spot something wrong in existing
code, can I fix it now or does it have to wait for later?

Code reviews. Deploying to test environments. Testing what I've made,
myself, before anyone else sees it.

After a production deploy, checking the logs and the performance to
make sure my changes actually work. **Definition of done:** it's not
done until it's running in production.

## 2. What do you enjoy most about your role?

Four things:

1. Solving problems no one else has cracked yet.
2. Working with smart people who teach me stuff.
3. The detective work of hunting down a bug or an incident,
   layer by layer, clue by clue.
4. When something finally works, especially after I've been
   trying to make it work for some time. The longer the wrestle,
   the bigger the kick when it clicks.

## 3. What are the biggest challenges of working in this career?

Two kinds: the work, and the people.

**On the work side:** meetings that should have been an email. Slow
systems. Fixing the same problem twice because we didn't fix the
root cause the first time.

**On the people side:** that's been harder for me than the tech,
honestly. Speaking up in a big meeting as an introvert. Saying no
when I'm already flat out. Giving honest feedback to someone I like.
None of that came naturally. Still working on it.

The good news: the people skills are learnable, just like the tech
skills. They just take longer because you can't practise them at
home with a laptop.

## 4. What qualifications, training, or experience did you need to get started?

**The formal qualification:** Bachelor of Computer Science with Honours at James
Cook University in Townsville. Three years + 1 year for Honours.

**The unofficial training started earlier.** In high school I knew
I liked computers. Played network games with my friends after school.
Went to the library and messed around with **Quick Basic**, a simple
programming language. The algorithm challenges always tripped me up,
but I kept going. That's where the actual learning started.

**The experience that made me employable** wasn't the degree by
itself. In second year of uni I wanted to do an internship, but
there wasn't much going in Townsville. My best friend and I started
our own company instead, a small startup. We took on small projects.
Never made much money. The experience was the point.

Kept the startup running all through uni. Three months after I
finished my degree, I wanted real income, so I left.

Got a job at an online travel agency that built travelthere.com (the
site doesn't exist anymore). Flight Centre bought the company. I've
been at FC ever since. 21 years.

**The lesson:** the degree opens the door. The hands-on practice
(Quick Basic in high school, startup projects through uni) is what
gets you through it. You need both, but the practice matters more
than people think.

**For ethical hacking specifically,** you don't strictly need a
degree. You need demonstrable skills (Capture The Flag wins,
write-ups, public GitHub repos) and you'll get further with one of:
a CS degree, a cyber security course at TAFE or uni, or industry
certs like CompTIA Security+ or OSCP later in your career.

## 5. What skills are most important for someone wanting to work in this industry?

**90% of programming is reading code.** Your code, other people's
code. Understanding it.

That surprises a lot of people. They picture engineers typing fast
all day. The truth is most of our time is spent reading: working out
what the previous person did, why they did it that way, and where to
plug the change in.

So the best skill to build now is **reading**. Code, definitely. But
also instructions, error messages, documentation, books. Strong
readers learn fast and get unstuck quickly.

A few more for any career:

- **Breaking a problem down into smaller problems.** Solve all the
  small problems and you've solved the big one. This is the heart
  of how engineers (and ethical hackers) think.
- **Writing.** Short sentences. One idea at a time. Clear writing
  comes from clear thinking.
- **Be curious.** Poke around. Test things. Try things. Play with
  things. That's how you learn what's really going on, and it's
  exactly how ethical hackers find what others have missed.
- **Ask why. Then ask why again.** The first answer is rarely the
  real answer. Keep asking until you hit the root cause. The same
  trick finds the real bug, the real motive, or the real reason a
  system was built the way it was.
- **Always write tests.** You'll never be able to test the whole
  system by hand. Three layers matter: **unit tests** (one function
  at a time), **integration tests** (a few pieces working together),
  and **end-to-end tests** (the whole thing the way a user would
  use it). Tests let you change one bit of code and know straight
  away if you broke something somewhere else.
- **Always compile and run the tests after every change, no matter
  how small.** A one-character typo can break the whole thing.
  Catch it now, while you still remember what you changed, not
  later when you don't.
- **Write comments that explain *why*, not *what*.** A bad comment
  is one that just rewrites the code as English. No one likes
  reading that. A good comment answers: *what problem does this
  solve? what breaks if you don't do it this way?* Your future
  self in six months will thank you.

For ethical hacking specifically:

- Learn **Python**. It's the language hackers and security people
  use most.
- **Keep learning languages.** Once you're confident with Python,
  move onto something else, for example JavaScript. Each new
  language teaches you something the last one didn't, and broadens
  what you can attack and defend. The **TIOBE Index**
  (tiobe.com/tiobe-index) is a good place to see what's most used
  right now. Don't ignore the older business languages either.
  **Java** (what I write in at Flight Centre) and **C#** (also
  called .NET) still run a huge amount of the world's banking,
  travel, healthcare, and government code. Newer languages get the
  headlines, but the code already running in Java and C# isn't
  going anywhere. Engineers who can work in those languages are
  always in demand.
- Learn **how networks work**. IP addresses, ports, HTTP, DNS.
- Try **Capture The Flag challenges**. Free sites like TryHackMe,
  HackTheBox, and PicoCTF. They teach you hacking legally, on
  practice systems.
- Read security write-ups. Find a bug bounty story you like and
  follow how the person found the bug.
- Learn about **Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVEs)**.
  These are publicly listed security bugs. Each one has an ID like
  CVE-2024-12345, plus a write-up of what the bug was, who found
  it, and how to fix it. Browse a few. Pick one that looks
  interesting and read the full story. That's how professional
  ethical hackers learn what to look for.
- **Write tests for every security fix you make.** Once you patch
  a vulnerability, write a test that proves it's fixed. The test
  stays in the codebase forever and makes sure that bug never
  comes back to haunt you. Same trick works for any bug you
  squash.

## 6. If you were my age again, what would you do to prepare for this career?

**Six things, in order:**

1. **Build things. Anything.** A website, a small game, a chatbot,
   a Python script that does something useful. The thing itself
   doesn't matter. The act of building does.
2. **Publish your code publicly on github.com.** Anything you do,
   put it up there. Any challenge you solve. Anything new you try.
   Employers look at your GitHub before your CV. A small public
   repo beats a polished resume.
3. **Do Capture The Flag competitions.** They build a portfolio
   for an ethical hacking career.
4. **Talk to people in the field.** Like today. Ask them how they
   got in. Most people are happy to share.
5. **Go to user groups.** Look up **meetup.com** for events near
   you. They're free local gatherings of people working in IT,
   security, a specific language, you name it. Show up, listen,
   ask questions. You'll meet engineers and hackers who'll happily
   point you to the next step.
6. **Get good marks where you can**, but don't stress if maths and
   English aren't your top subjects. Hands-on projects matter more.

**Four things I wish someone had told me at your age:**

- **It's OK to start small.** My first car was a 1994 Suzuki Swift.
  Cheap, three cylinders, got me to work. The car I actually wanted
  came later, once I had the income. Same idea with jobs: a basic
  first role gets you in the door.
- **Asking for help is a strength.** People who never ask get stuck.
  People who ask, learn faster.
- **The people you work with matter more than the job title.** A
  good team beats a fancy title every time.
- **You don't need to know what you want to be.** Pick a direction
  and start. You'll work it out as you go.

## 7. What opportunities are available for growth and advancement in this field?

Three directions, and you don't have to pick now.

**1. The technical track.** Junior Engineer, Mid-level, Senior,
Staff, Principal. You go deeper into the craft. Build harder things,
solve harder problems, mentor others. Salary roughly doubles from
Junior to Senior, and again from Senior to Principal. This is the
path I'm on.

**2. The leadership track.** Team Lead, Engineering Manager,
Engineering Director, Chief Technology Officer (CTO). You stop
writing code full-time and start leading people. Different job, same
career. Some engineers love it. Some hate it. You don't know which
one you are until you try.

**3. Specialism.** Pick a niche that fascinates you and go deep.
**Ethical hacking and security** is one of the most in-demand
specialisms right now. So is **AI**, **DevOps** (what I do now),
**data engineering**, and **cloud architecture**. Specialists tend
to get paid more than generalists once they're senior, because
there are fewer of them.

You can also move sideways between specialisms. I worked as a
Software Engineer for nearly 20 years, then moved into DevOps
full-time. Same company. The skills carried across. Sideways moves
are how you stay interested over a long career.

For ethical hacking specifically, the career ladder includes:
Junior Penetration Tester, Senior Pen Tester, Red Team Lead,
Security Architect, Chief Information Security Officer (CISO).
And there's the freelance bug bounty path where the best in the
world earn six figures finding bugs for companies like Google,
Apple, and Microsoft.

## 8. What is something people often misunderstand about your job?

**For ethical hacking, the biggest one: people think hackers are
criminals.** Movies and TV have a lot to answer for. The reality is
that ethical hackers are paid professionals who help companies find
their security holes before the criminals do.

The industry uses less loaded job titles in the ads. You'll see:

- **Penetration Tester** (Pen Tester). Tests systems and
  applications for vulnerabilities, with permission.
- **Bug Bounty Hunter**. Finds bugs in big companies' systems
  through their public bug bounty programs. Paid per bug.
- **Security Researcher**. Discovers and responsibly reports new
  vulnerabilities.
- **Red Team Operator**. Simulates real-world attackers to test a
  company's defences.
- **Application Security Engineer** (AppSec). Works inside an
  engineering team to keep code secure as it's being written.
- **Vulnerability Analyst**. Reviews scanner output, hunts for
  weaknesses across an organisation's systems, and helps engineers
  prioritise the fixes.

Same skill set, different angle. All respected, in high demand,
and well paid. You're the good guy.

**For software engineering more broadly: people think it's a solo
job done in silence.** Some of it is. A lot isn't. Pair programming,
code reviews, design discussions, helping a teammate who's stuck.
The introvert-in-a-hoodie stereotype is real for some engineers,
but most of us spend a real chunk of the day talking to other
humans about the problem at hand.

**And one more, because it surprises every student I talk to: you
don't need to be a maths genius.** I'm not. I use maths very
rarely. A real example from the last few years: calculating sector
prices on an air fare. Take the total amount, work out the GST,
back out the base amount, then split it across each sector of the
trip. Basic arithmetic on a small set of numbers.

It's mostly formulas, and the computer does the actual calculation.
I don't even have to remember the formulas. I look them up when I
need them. The real skill is knowing what to ask, not memorising
the answer.

If you can follow a recipe and spot when something doesn't add up,
you've got the maths you need.

## 9. What advice would you give to someone considering this career path?

If you genuinely love a puzzle, this is one of the best careers
going. I get a real buzz when I solve something, especially when
other people couldn't crack it. That feeling is what keeps me
coming back to the job 21 years in. There's a saying I like:

> *"Find a job you love and you will never work a day in your life."*

That's how it is for me. I love a puzzle. I love making things
work. I love seeing something I built help someone get through
their day better. I get paid to play with computers and solve
problems with smart people. That's a good deal. Find work that
gives you that feeling and hold onto it.

**Here's what I'd tell you about the work itself.**

The character stuff matters more than the IQ stuff. The best
engineers I've worked with aren't always the smartest. They're the
most reliable. Bosses and employers look for these things, and they
apply to any career:

- **You show up.** On time. Every day. No big deal made of it.
- **You finish what you start.** Even when it gets boring.
- **You're honest when you don't know something.** Saying *"I don't
  know, I'll find out"* is a strength, not a weakness.
- **You help others.** Share what you learn. Make the team better.
- **You keep your word.** If you say you'll do it by Friday, do it
  by Friday.

Notice how little of that is about being the smartest person in
the room.

**The single best piece of advice I've ever been given:**

> *"Show up."*

That's it. Consistency beats brilliance. The person who comes back
next week, every week, gets further than the genius who turns up
once.

We've heard this throughout the IBFF program too. **Consistent is
better than perfect.** Same lesson.

## 10. Is there anyone else you would recommend I speak with, podcasts I should listen to, books I should read, or training platforms I should follow?

**People to speak with:**

- Any other mentor or speaker you've met through IBFF. Ask them
  the same questions you asked me.
- Your teachers, especially the IT and maths ones. They'll know
  the local short courses and uni open days.
- Professionals on LinkedIn. Send a polite message saying you're
  a student exploring the field. Most people working in IT and
  security are happy to spend 15 minutes with a curious teenager.
  The worst they can say is no.

**People to follow:**

- **Troy Hunt** (troyhunt.com). Australian security expert. I
  watched his Pluralsight course *Hack Yourself First: How to Go
  on the Cyber-Offense* and my mind was blown. He posts shorter
  free videos on YouTube too, but the full Pluralsight course is
  the one to access if you can. Worth the subscription on its
  own.

**Podcasts:**

- **Darknet Diaries** (darknetdiaries.com). True stories from the
  dark side of the internet. Real hackers, real cases.
  Brilliantly produced by Jack Rhysider.
- **Risky Business** (risky.biz). Weekly security news, hosted by
  Patrick Gray. Australian host, so the timezones work in your
  favour.

**Books:**

- **The Pragmatic Programmer** by Andy Hunt and Dave Thomas. The
  book every engineer wishes they'd read at your age.
- **Clean Code** by Robert C. Martin. How to write code other
  people (and future-you) can read.
- **The Phoenix Project** by Gene Kim, Kevin Behr, and George
  Spafford. A novel about an IT manager who takes on a failing
  project. The book that introduced DevOps thinking to a wide
  audience.
- **The Unicorn Project** by Gene Kim. Follow-up to The Phoenix
  Project, told from a developer's perspective. About what
  happens when engineers are given the time and tools to do
  their best work.
- **Ghost in the Wires** by Kevin Mitnick. Autobiography of one of
  the world's most famous (formerly criminal, now reformed)
  hackers. Reads like a thriller.
- **Mastermind: How to Think Like Sherlock Holmes** by Maria
  Konnikova. How to train yourself to notice details, slow down,
  and reason from evidence. Useful for any kind of detective
  work, including hunting down bugs and incidents.

**Training platforms:**

- **TryHackMe** (tryhackme.com). The best place to start ethical
  hacking. Step-by-step guided rooms. Free tier is generous.
- **HackTheBox** (hackthebox.com). Harder, less hand-holding. Move
  here once TryHackMe feels easy.
- **PicoCTF** (picoctf.org). Free CTF challenges run by Carnegie
  Mellon University. Designed for students.
- **freeCodeCamp** (freecodecamp.org). Free full curriculum for
  web development. Earns you certificates as you go.
- **Secure Code Warrior** (securecodewarrior.com). The platform I
  use at work. Teaches secure coding by showing you vulnerable
  code and asking you to spot the bug. There are some free
  challenges you can try straight away. For broader access, your
  school might be able to get you a student account.

**Communities (free, in person):**

- **Brisbane Cyber Security** meetup
  (meetup.com/meetup-group-agfkzpmt). A local group for people
  working and learning in cyber security in Brisbane. Join up,
  then work through the previous talk recordings members can
  access. A great way to learn from years of local talks before
  you ever attend in person.
- **meetup.com** generally. Search Brisbane for "Python",
  "JavaScript", "hacking", or "developer" to find more groups.
  Most welcome students.

**Things to follow online:**

- The **CVE database** (cve.mitre.org). New publicly listed
  security bugs every day.
- Security write-ups on **HackerOne**, **Bugcrowd**, and **Snyk**.
  Real bug bounty stories and vulnerability research.
- The **TIOBE Index** (tiobe.com/tiobe-index) for which programming
  languages are most used right now.

# Bonus material if the conversation goes there

## What do you do when you have a tough day?

- Go for a run with the FC Run Club, or by myself if no one's around.
- Take my lunch into the South Bank Parklands, find a quiet table,
  watch a movie and eat my lunch.
- Talk to my Team Lead about it.
- Talk to my wife about it when I get home.
- Sleep on it.

If something is still bothering me in the morning, then it's a real
problem and I deal with it. Most things look smaller after a night's
sleep.

**One habit that helps a lot:** whenever an answer pops into my head,
I write it down. On a run, in the shower, in the middle of the
night, doesn't matter. The best ideas come when you're not at your
desk. Nothing worse than knowing you'd thought of the answer and
then forgotten it by morning.

## What's one mistake you learned from?

Honest answer first: this question is hard for me, because I don't
tend to remember the bad stuff. That's part of why I'm usually happy
and positive.

There's one from about a year ago though, when I first moved into
DevOps full time.

Our build system went down. New virtual machines weren't being
created automatically. We should have had 16 running. We had 2.

I was really struggling to fix the scaling problem (getting new
instances to spin up). Out of frustration, I stopped and deleted
the 2 working instances.

Now no one could build. No one could deploy. The production deploy
had to be postponed. I was super embarrassed.

Lesson: when you've got 2 working instances, do not stop them. Wait
for help. Get support on the scaling problem first, with the
working ones still up.

**The bigger lesson:** when you're frustrated, stop and ask. Don't make
the problem worse by yourself.

**What did I do?** I worked out how to fix the problem. Then I documented it.
Then I scripted it. Now, when this happens again, I run my script and problem solved!!
5 seconds.

**Everything is an opportunity to improve.** We can never automate too much.

## What's changing in your industry right now?

Two big things.

### AI

AI is **assisting** engineers, not replacing them. That's my view.

There are some companies generating too much code with AI and not
checking it properly. I expect those companies will be hiring
engineers in a couple of years to come in and clean up the mess.

I use **Claude Code** every day myself. I've gone a step further and
written my own *skills* for Claude (small instruction packs) so it
writes docs and code in my style without me having to tell it each
time. The point: don't fear the tool, learn how to make it work for
you.

### Security

Security keeps getting more serious. Every company gets attacked.

At work I use **Secure Code Warrior**. It teaches me secure coding:
how to spot vulnerabilities, how to fix them, and how to prevent
getting hacked in the first place. It also teaches you to think like
a hacker, so you can test your own security fixes.

That last part is exactly what ethical hacking does for a living:
think like the attacker, then prove the defender's work is solid.
People who can do both are in demand and well paid.
